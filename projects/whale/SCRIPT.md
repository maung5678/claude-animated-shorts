# "Raining Whale" — original script v1 (2026-11-07)
Format: cartoon-3d-short (~60 s, ~3.7 words/s), one steady voice, our own characters + story.
Facts (sources below):
- Nov 1970, beach just south of Florence, Oregon: a dead sperm whale, ~45 ft / ~8 tons, washed ashore and rotted.
- Oregon beaches were legally highways, so the Oregon State Highway Division got the job; engineer George Thornton.
- Plan: blast it into pieces small enough for scavengers (seagulls) to eat. Used 20 cases, ~half a ton (1,000 lb) of dynamite.
- Spectator Walter Umenhofer, WWII-era military explosives experience, warned it was far too much dynamite.
- ~75 spectators and a KATU TV crew (reporter Paul Linnman) watched from a distance; blubber rained down on them.
- A large chunk of blubber crushed Umenhofer's new car, bought in a dealer promotion called "a whale of a deal".
  The state paid him for it. (Make varies by source: not named in the script.)
- Most of the carcass remained; scavengers were scared off; the crew buried the rest.
- 1979: 41 sperm whales stranded near Florence; dynamite was passed over, they were buried.
- 2020: Florence, by popular vote, named the Exploding Whale Memorial Park.

In 1970, Oregon tried to get rid of a dead whale with half a ton of dynamite.
A forty-five foot sperm whale had washed up on a beach near Florence, and it was starting to stink.
In Oregon, the beach counted as a highway, so the job went to the highway department.
Their plan: blow it into pieces so small the seagulls would clean up the rest.
A man in the crowd who knew explosives from the army said that was way too much.
The engineers used the full half ton anyway.
The crowd was moved back, the TV cameras rolled, and someone pushed the button.
Then it started raining whale.
Chunks of blubber came down on the dunes, the cars, and the people.
Luckily, nobody got hurt.
Unluckily, the man who warned them had just bought a brand new car, in a sale called "A Whale of a Deal".
One chunk flattened it.
You see, a blast doesn't break things into neat little pieces. It pushes out wherever it's easiest to go.
Ever stomped on a ketchup packet? It doesn't spread out nicely, it shoots out one side.
The sand pushed back, so the blast threw the whale up and over everyone.
Most of the whale was still there, and the seagulls never came back.
Nine years later, forty-one whales washed up on the same beach, and nobody even mentioned dynamite.
And in 2020, the town voted to name a park after it: Exploding Whale Memorial Park.
